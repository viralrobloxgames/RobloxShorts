# Auto Shorts Story

Write an original spoken script that is interesting to hear and practical to show in low-poly animation. The member's idea, changed lines and requested length take precedence over the defaults below.

## Default deliverable

For "give me a script," return one complete narration, with short paragraphs and no shot labels, timestamps, emoji or production directions inside the spoken body. Include source notes separately when factual claims require them. If the member asks for two stories, deliver two distinct complete scripts. For a narrow line change, change that line; when asked for the whole script, provide the full revised version.

A useful default is about 120–170 spoken words for a roughly 40–60-second Short. Actual timing depends on delivery and must be measured when audio exists. Do not pad to an exact word count or promise an exact runtime from text alone. Offer alternatives only when asked or when the premise is materially undecided.

## Pick the story mode from the request

- **Power with a downside:** establish what the power does and the natural expectation, then expose one clear limitation through action. Default to second person. A power can work exactly as stated and still create trouble.
- **Grounded action:** put the person into a concrete situation immediately. Show an attempted solution, a worsening obstacle or misunderstood action, and a payoff that changes what the opening meant.
- **Factual what-if:** introduce an interesting physical situation, explain the relevant mechanism in plain language, then show consequences without inventing thresholds, certainty or documented outcomes.

Do not force every story into a superhero premise. For everyday interactions, let people react to each other and give their actions consequences.

## Hook and progression

Start with the situation or the power. Make the second line sharpen the expectation or danger. Avoid greetings, "today we will," long backstory and repeated "picture this" or "imagine." If the member specifies "you walk into an arena," open there rather than explaining the topic first.

Prefer concrete verbs: the truck swerves, the gate shuts, your shoe hits the pavement, someone grabs the strap. Each new beat should change location, danger, knowledge or the available choice. Give the animation something meaningful to show. Short sentences help pace action; vary their length so the narration does not become a list of fragments.

Use one understandable causal rule and keep it consistent. Make attempts solve part of the problem while leaving another problem exposed. Do not invent a last-second exception just to force a twist. End on a visible consequence, an earned reversal, or a concise line that reframes the opening. Avoid a generic moral, engagement demand or explanation after the payoff unless requested.

## Learned writing preferences

- Use "you" for immersive hypotheticals when appropriate.
- In documentary/action scripts, omit a person's name when it delays the story and is not necessary for understanding. Keep needed attribution/facts in separate notes.
- A stronger second line matters; revise it without derailing the rest of the story.
- Avoid repeating framing words in adjacent lines. If the member flags a phrase, carry that correction through the complete script.
- Make people interact and let obstacles escalate through actions rather than a lecture.
- Keep the final reaction or realization long enough to register in production.

Read the examples and revision patterns below when matching the lesson's story mechanics. Use the examples to understand causality, not to repeat the same script for every idea.

## Factual boundaries and handoff

For real events, animal capabilities, food/health effects or physical claims, verify relevant facts with reliable current sources. Distinguish fiction, illustrative staging and speculative outcomes from documented facts. Do not turn myths such as exaggerated animal strength or a precise dangerous food threshold into certainty for a stronger hook. Put concise sources outside the narration; the voice script should remain clean.

If the member supplies a final recording, do not rewrite the words for an unrelated animation task. If asked only for a script, deliver the script without starting voice generation, rendering, scheduling or publication. For a full video request, hand the finalized narration and premise to the available animation workflow; this story skill alone does not create the MP4.

# Story mechanics from the course

## The Lesson 2 example

Fictional rule: teleportation changes location but preserves momentum.

The viewer expects teleportation to end a crash. The character escapes the car, yet is still moving along the street. A second jump avoids a van but adds a fall. The consequence comes from the original rule instead of an unrelated surprise.

The closing thought is that leaving the car and losing its speed are separate problems. Preserve this kind of causal clarity in a new idea, not the exact sequence of vehicles and jumps.

## Other useful shapes

- **Indestructible body:** the character cannot be injured, but still has ordinary mass and can be pushed or dragged. Show that distinction with an attempted stop and a visible failure.
- **Ten-second rewind:** a rescue attempt changes the danger for another person. Repeated attempts reveal that preventing the situation is more effective than chasing each consequence. The clue needs to be visible before the resolution.
- **Misunderstood intervention:** one person grabs another's backpack; it looks like a theft until a trapped strap explains the urgency. Actions and reactions change the viewer's understanding.
- **Creature comparison:** begin in the encounter and explain only the traits that affect it. Do not present a speculative fight as a proven result or invent casualty counts.

These are fictional or hypothetical scenarios unless separately sourced. Use original staging for a new script. Do not encourage a real-world attempt at a dangerous stunt.

## Revision examples

Weak: "Picture it. Imagine you are standing at the front."
Stronger: "You are at the front. Ninety-nine people behind you."

Weak: "Teleportation is very useful, but it has disadvantages."
Stronger: "The sidewalk appears beneath your feet. But you are still moving at seventy miles an hour."

Weak: "The lesson is that every superpower has a cost."
Stronger: End on the specific consequence this power created, using words or action that connect back to the opening.
