# Dick Snifford episodes: research notes (studied locally, 5 Oct 2026)

Reference for writing the next "Detective Max Sniffwell" episode. Seven episodes of the AI detective parody "Dick Snifford" (Instagram @dicksnifford, created by logicbent) were studied in a signed-in desktop browser. "The Pimp Case" was skipped as instructed.

## Read this first: how these notes were made, and their limits

- **Source used:** the YouTube uploads (channel `UCrVPTecdrgEUHX52ppBpARA`), because YouTube exposes a caption track for every episode. Instagram was used for the view, like and comment counts only (the browser was not logged in to Instagram, but the Reels tab and counts were visible).
- **Audio was not heard.** The session that made these notes cannot listen to sound. Dialogue comes from YouTube's caption track for each video (auto-generated for six episodes; The Jacuzzi Case has clean, punctuated captions that look creator-supplied). Music and sound effects are only noted where the captions tag them (`[music]`, `[screaming]`, `[applause]`, `[snorts]`, `[laughter]`). Voice tone, music style and exact SFX are **unknown**.
- **Pictures were sampled, not watched in motion.** Each video was stepped through and a frame was grabbed every 2.5 s (every 0.5 s around some title cards). Timestamps for visuals are therefore accurate to roughly ±1–2 s. Camera moves are inferred from consecutive frames (for example a wide shot followed by an extreme close-up of the same subject), so "crash zoom" versus "hard cut to close-up" cannot be told apart.
- **Who is speaking** is inferred from the frame on screen at the caption's timestamp and from context. Lines marked **VO** are the detective's voiceover (he is not shown talking, and the line is past-tense or reflective). Where attribution is a guess it says so.
- **Dialogue is paraphrased, not transcribed.** These are someone else's scripts, so the lines below are written in our own words, line by line, keeping meaning, order, length and joke mechanics. The only line quoted exactly is the catchphrase already given in the brief: "Tempting. But I'm on a case." Swearing is replaced with `[swear]`.
- **No screenshots are saved** in this folder. Frames of the videos are the creator's copyrighted material, so title cards and key frames are described in words instead.
- Counts were read on 5 Oct 2026.

## The world in one paragraph

1970s Miami, shot like a sun-faded TV cop show. Dick Snifford is a lanky blond detective with an enormous nose, a pink-and-grey patterned short-sleeved shirt, white trousers, white loafers, a brown leather shoulder holster and a badge on a chain round his neck. He wears the same outfit in every episode. Recurring cast: the **Chief** (dark-haired man, navy suit, badge on the breast pocket; calm, corrupt, always mid-leisure) and an **older uniformed officer** (moustache, light-blue shirt, peaked cap) who delivers the summons or the evidence. Everything else changes per episode.

### Hook (first 3 seconds)
A woman at a beach bar opens with a pickup line on the detective, and he answers by rating it. No case, no crime, no title. Flirt first.

### Title card
- **When:** appears at about 0:26, gone by 0:31 (roughly 4.5 s). This is unusually late: three scenes (flirt, summons, briefing) come first.
- **What it says:** small white "Detective" above large yellow "DICK SNIFFORD". No episode name in the video itself.
- **Where:** lower-left third of the frame, left-aligned.
- **Behind it:** the detective's back as he walks down the photo-lined corridor to meet the candidate.

### Structure
- **Setup:** kidnapped wife, ransom, an election today. The Chief's motive is political, not moral.
- **Complication reveal:** the candidate is literally a clown, and nobody remarks on it.
- **Clue:** the ransom note smells of a specific taco.
- **Suspects / red herring:** the "kidnappers" in the taco joint are real, but the kidnap is not.
- **Temptation:** the flirt at the top, cut short by duty.
- **Twist:** the wife ran off willingly with Diego and invented the ransom to pay for earrings.
- **Second turn:** Dick returns her anyway because the job is to win the election.
- **Ending:** the system gets what it wanted, the detective gets beaten up and a beer.

### Running gags and catchphrases
- Opening on a flirt that is interrupted by the job.
- The Chief briefing from a leisure activity (here: putting on a dock).
- A smell identified in wine-taster language.
- Closing voiceover that states a cynical life lesson.
- "@logicbent" hidden on a prop in the last shot.

### How the nose is used
1. Romantic: he leans in with the nose first.
2. Forensic: one sniff of the note gives the exact restaurant.
3. Slapstick: food stuck on the nose through a whole surveillance scene, paid off at a mirror during the tense approach.

### Ending
- **Last line (VO, paraphrased):** he has a beer and three punches to the face, and democracy is some system.
- **Final shot:** battered detective at the bar, then the logicbent dial tag.
- **Call to action:** none spoken or on screen.

### Three most effective moments
1. **The clown reveal (0:37).** The briefing sets up a serious politician; the cut to a clown in a business suit lands because everyone treats it as normal. The follow-up question, whether "it" is working, is funnier than the reveal.
2. **Sour cream on the nose (1:10–1:35).** A visual gag that runs silently under a tension scene and pays off at the mirror. It needs no dialogue, so it works with sound off.
3. **The wife's speech (2:28).** The same sentence the candidate used as a slogan comes back from her with a different meaning. A callback that closes the plot.

### Adult flags and clean version
- Mild flirting (fine). Guns, a man thrown from a window, a black eye. One mild insult. No swearing in captions. The speech line has a faint double meaning.
- **Clean take:** a candidate for class president or server mayor who campaigns in a silly costume; his "kidnapped" running mate actually left to join the rival team and faked the ransom note to get Robux for a hat. The detective drags them back for the vote anyway and gets nothing but a bonk for it.

### Hook (first 3 seconds)
A loud night pool party with the detective dancing. Pure spectacle, no dialogue. The premise (hangover) is set up visually before any plot.

### Title card
- **When:** roughly 0:09–0:11 (about 2.5 s), straight after the blackout.
- **Text / position:** same logo, lower-left.
- **Behind it:** quiet, wide morning-after bedroom. The contrast with the party is the joke.

### Structure
- **Setup:** hung-over detective, dead vampire, nobody cares.
- **Clue:** garlic on the breath, found by nose.
- **Character evidence:** the flashbacks show the "monster" was the nicest man in town. This flips who the audience roots for.
- **Suspect:** the car tailing him turns out to be a vampire hunter who simply walks up and confesses by asking for help.
- **Temptation:** drink (he wants one from the first line) and, at the end, the brides.
- **Twist 1:** the detective protects the vampires from the hunter. **Twist 2:** the victim was never permanently dead and gets up. **Twist 3:** the vampire leaves anyway, hurt by how he was treated.
- **Ending:** the detective is carried off by the supernatural and decides not to write it down.

### Running gags and catchphrases
- Chief summons by telephone; Chief has a drink on his desk.
- The detective misreads an obvious clue (stakes as fence posts) while his nose reads a subtle one perfectly.
- Closing VO built as a simile ("X is like...") followed by a shrug.
- The officer who delivers deadpan bureaucratic facts.
- The catchphrase from the brief does **not** appear in this episode.

### How the nose is used
One long, uncomfortable sniff directly at the corpse's mouth, followed by a single-word finding. The length of the hold is the joke. The nose also leads every profile two-shot (secretary, Chief).

### Ending
- **Last line (VO, paraphrased):** whatever happened next was not going in the report.
- **Final shot:** his feet floating off the floor, then the mallet tag.
- **Call to action:** none.

### Three most effective moments
1. **"Fence installer" (1:51).** The best joke-per-second in the set: a prop (two stakes), one misunderstanding, done. It also characterises him as brilliant by nose and dim by eye.
2. **The kind-vampire flashbacks (1:17).** Ten seconds that reverse the audience's sympathy and make the later twist feel earned.
3. **Floating loafers (2:48).** The ending is told through one odd image while the VO understates it. Picture and narration disagree, which is the series' strongest closing device.

### Adult flags and clean version
- Alcohol throughout (party shots, hangover, bar, Chief's whisky). One swear from the blind man and mild "hell". He wakes up in bed beside someone (clothed, nothing shown). Brides in nightgowns; implied seduction at the end. Immigration satire. A corpse and a staking attempt.
- **Clean take:** the detective is groggy from an all-night obby marathon or too much birthday cake, not drink. The "victim" is a friendly vampire-avatar player found knocked out by a garlic-bread item. Flashbacks show him helping noobs. The "hunter" hands over two stakes and the detective thinks they are tent pegs. The vampire respawns, and leaves the server because nobody was nice to him.

## Patterns across episodes

### Length
- Range 1:33 to 3:00. Four of seven sit between 2:40 and 3:00.
- The two shortest (Jacuzzi 1:33, Great American 1:44) are the simplest: one idea, one twist.
- The best performer by far is the shortest. Longer episodes add suspects and set pieces but did not earn more views.

### How each episode opens
- Always a **cold open before the title**, and never with the crime being explained by narration.
- Four of seven open on **flirting or a date** (Jacuzzi, Election, Love, Cambridge). Two open on spectacle with no detective dialogue (Vampire party, Kickoff locker room). One opens on a conversation (Great American).
- The detective's face, and therefore the nose, is on screen within the first 3 s in five of seven.
- The first spoken line is usually said **to** him by someone else, and his reply is the first joke.

### Title card format
- Identical every time: small white "Detective" over large yellow slab-serif "DICK SNIFFORD" with a dark drop shadow, **lower-left**, over live footage, never on a blank background.
- On screen for about 2.5–4.5 s.
- Timing: 0:06 (Jacuzzi), 0:07 (Cambridge), 0:09 (Vampire), 0:17 (Great American), 0:24 (Love), 0:26 (Election), 0:27 (Kickoff). Later episodes pushed it back to nearly half a minute; the hit had it at 6 s.
- It usually sits on a nose-forward close-up of the detective or on the first shot of the new location, and can bridge a cut.
- **The episode name is not in the video.** It is only on the cover: the same logo with "The ___ Case" in white serif beneath, over a character portrait (often a guest character, not the detective).
- Naming rule: always "The [one or two words] Case".

### How cases escalate
1. A summons (officer on foot, phone call, or the Chief found at play).
2. A briefing where the Chief's real concern is politics, money or his own comfort.
3. A single clue, read by nose in one beat (when the nose is used).
4. A drive in the convertible to a new location, often under voiceover.
5. Two or three odd characters, each introduced with a visual gag before they speak.
6. A confrontation that turns physical for about 10 s (gun drawn, scuffle, brawl).
7. A reversal, then a short coda somewhere completely different (bar, boat, sofa, plane, pool).

A stated countdown (Kickoff) or a rule of three (Jacuzzi, Great American, the firing montage in Love) is what holds the middle together.

### How the twists work
- **The victim is not a victim:** the kidnapped wife left on purpose; the dead vampire gets up.
- **The culprit is the least threatening figure:** the mascot, the dinner lady.
- **Authority is the real problem:** the Chief is bought (Jacuzzi), wants a result for political reasons (Election), or the coach forgives the crime because he needs a player (Kickoff).
- **The detective solves it and loses anyway:** beaten up, dumped, abducted, exhausted. Or he solves it and sells out.
- **A second, smaller twist in the coda** contradicts the calm narration (floating feet, spy on the plane, speedboat).

### Endings
- Every episode ends on the **detective's voiceover**: two sentences, the first a general truth or self-description, the second a dry reversal.
- He is nearly always alone or detached in the last scene, with a drink, food or a view.
- No episode has a spoken or written call to action. No "follow for part 2". The only branding is "@logicbent" or "by logicbent" hidden on a prop in the final shot (boat hull, mallet, football, radio dial, plane livery). Two episodes showed no tag in the sampled frames.
- Episodes are self-contained. Nothing carries over except the cast and the outfit.

### Recurring gags and lines across episodes
- **"Tempting. But I'm on a case."** appears only in The Jacuzzi Case among these seven, three times with the third cut short. It is the series' signature, but it is not in every episode.
- The detective is propositioned or flirted with, and something stops it.
- The Chief is never at a desk doing work: golf on a dock, binoculars at a rocket launch, sun lounger, whisky.
- The older uniformed officer as the straight man who states facts.
- Smell findings delivered like a wine tasting ("notes of...").
- The convertible driving shot under voiceover.
- The unchanged outfit in every setting, including England and a tank.
- Brilliant nose, poor common sense (stakes taken for fence posts; cannot remember why he flew to England).
- The name "Bobby" for incidental characters.

Rules that seem to hold:
- **One decisive sniff per case**, early (0:22 in the hit), and it is always right.
- The sniff is shot as an **extreme close-up**, held longer than is comfortable, with a bystander reacting.
- The result is either one blunt word or a tasting-note list of three items that ends on something personal or silly.
- The nose is also a prop for slapstick (sour cream), for romance (leaning in, a finger stroking it) and for composition (profile two-shots, title card).
- The three episodes with no sniff are among the four weakest on Instagram.

### What performs best, and what the winners share
Instagram views, studied episodes only: Jacuzzi 2.8M, Vampire 820K, Love 226K, Cambridge 127K, Great American 115K, Kickoff 59.5K. (Election is not on Instagram. The skipped Pimp Case has 2.2M.) YouTube is tiny by comparison and follows roughly the same order: Jacuzzi 4.7K, Election 3.6K, Vampire 2.9K, Great American 2.0K, Love 0.9K, Kickoff 0.7K, Cambridge 0.3K.

Likes and comments follow views closely. Jacuzzi leads everything (72K likes, 2,440 comments on Instagram). Election has the most YouTube comments (12).

What the top episodes have in common:
- **They came first.** Views fall almost steadily with each later release, so some of the gap is novelty and early algorithm luck, not quality. Treat the ranking with caution.
- **The nose does real work.** Jacuzzi, Vampire and Election all hinge on a sniff.
- **A fast, clear hook with the detective on screen** and the title card early (6 s and 9 s for the top two).
- **A strong, simple high concept** that fits in the cover image: a detective in a hot tub, a vampire, a clown candidate.
- **A cynical but tidy twist** about people in charge.
- The hit is also the **shortest and has the catchphrase**.

What the weak ones share: no sniffing (Love, Great American, Cambridge), a late title card, gross-out as the main joke (Kickoff), a long detour unrelated to the case (the Cambridge brawl), or sponsorship (Cambridge).

## Takeaways for "Detective Max Sniffwell"

1. **Use The Jacuzzi Case skeleton:** flirt or invitation and catchphrase by second 5; title at about 6 s for about 3 s, lower-left over a nose close-up; case and stakes by 0:20; one sniff that solves it by 0:30; scent described as a three-item list; confrontation; the same invitation again; a twist from the boss; a third temptation where the catchphrase breaks; ironic narration over a contradicting final image.
2. **Aim for 75–100 s.** The series' own data favours short.
3. **Give Max a catchphrase and use it three times**, with the third use changed.
4. **Make the sniff the centrepiece:** extreme close-up, a beat too long, a bystander's face, then the tasting-note line. Do it once, early, and let it be right.
5. **Plant the culprit as a background joke** before anyone suspects them (the mascot).
6. **Give the boss a silly leisure activity** in every briefing and a selfish reason for wanting the case closed.
7. **End on narration that understates what the last picture shows.** Two sentences. No call to action inside the story; put ours after it or in the caption.
8. **Keep one outfit** in every setting.
9. **Try a visible countdown** if the episode runs past 90 s.
10. **Do not skip the nose.** The no-sniff episodes are the ones that underperformed.
