Mode: true story told as grounded action. Hook: the first speeding charge, at 8 mph. Rule: the 1860s-90s
"locomotive" laws made early cars absurdly slow (2 mph in town, a crew of three, someone walking ahead). Escalation:

## The facts (sources below)
- 28 January 1896, Paddock Wood, Kent: Walter Arnold drove his "horse-less carriage" at about 8 mph where the limit
  was 2 mph ("four times the speed limit") (Guinness World Records; Historic UK).
- A police constable chased him on a bicycle for about five miles and caught him (Guinness; Historic UK).
- Charged on four counts: using a locomotive without a horse on a public road, having fewer than three persons in
  charge of it, travelling faster than 2 mph, and not displaying his name and address (Guinness; Historic UK). He was
  convicted. Fine amounts differ between sources (speeding: 1 shilling + 9 shillings costs, or 10 shillings), so the
  script gives no amount. (Guinness also mentions a 28-day licence forfeit; driving licences didn't exist yet in
  1896, so that detail is left out.)
- The law: the Locomotive Acts required a crew of three, one walking ahead (the red flag of the 1865 Act became
  optional in 1878, but the person walking ahead remained); 2 mph in towns, 4 mph in the country (Historic UK,
  Guinness). Script: "one of them has to walk out in front".
- Later in 1896 (Locomotives on Highways Act, in force 14 November 1896) the walker requirement was dropped and the
  limit rose to 14 mph (Historic UK; Guinness: "Speed limit raised to 14 mph; the flag-bearer requirement was
  eliminated"). Drivers celebrated with the "Emancipation Run" from London to Brighton (Historic UK), commemorated
  by the London to Brighton Veteran Car Run held every November (verify the yearly-run line against the event's own
  site before production).
