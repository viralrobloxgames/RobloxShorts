On 20 March 1948 a tornado nobody saw coming tore across Tinker Air Force Base in Oklahoma at about 10 p.m., destroying around fifty planes. Back then tornadoes were called impossible to predict, and the U.S. Weather Bureau had banned public tornado warnings since the 1880s.

The base commander, Maj. Gen. Fred Borum, asked his two weather officers, Maj. Ernest Fawbush and Capt. Robert Miller, why they could forecast rain but not tornadoes, and told them to find out how. Five days later, after lunch on 25 March, they saw almost exactly the same weather pattern. Miller later said the odds of another tornado hitting the same spot "must have been billions to one." The general asked: "Are we going to have another tornado or not? Yes or no?"

They said yes. At 2:50 p.m. they issued the first official tornado forecast. Planes went into hangars, the rest were tied down, and people took shelter. Miller went home sure he'd be wrong, wondering if he could make a living as an elevator operator. Just before 6 p.m. a tornado hit the base again, almost exactly where the first one had. This time they were ready.

Their method went on to become modern tornado forecasting, and the ban on tornado warnings was lifted in 1950.
