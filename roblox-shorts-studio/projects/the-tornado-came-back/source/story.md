## The facts (kept accurate; sources below)
- Tinker Air Force Base, Oklahoma City, Oklahoma. Weathermen: Maj. Ernest J. Fawbush (33) and Capt. Robert C. Miller
  (28). Commanding officer: Maj. Gen. Fred S. Borum.
- 20 March 1948, about 10 pm (NWS 10:10 pm, DVIDS 10:22 pm): an unforecast tornado crossed the base. NWS: 54 aircraft
  destroyed (17 C-54s, 15 P-47s, 2 B-29s ...) and about 50 damaged, about 100 vehicles; $10.25 million; 8 injured, no
  deaths. DVIDS: 50 total losses and 50 repairable; Fox Weather: 52 demolished. Narration rounds to "fifty planes".
- A tribunal/inquiry ruled it "an act of God" and "not forecastable given the present state of the art" (Wikipedia,
  Tornado warning). Narration: "Tornadoes were called impossible to predict."
- The word: in 1885 the Signal Service's forecasters were prohibited from using "tornado" in forecasts; the Weather
  Bureau's ban on public tornado warnings was lifted only on 12 July 1950 (Wikipedia, Tornado warning). So in 1948
  "the weather service wasn't even allowed to use the word" holds (the Air Force weathermen weren't bound by it).
- 21 March: Borum questioned the weathermen and asked "if they could forecast rain, why couldn't they forecast
  tornadoes?" (DVIDS, paraphrased in the article). He refused to accept it as fate and ordered them to work out how to
  forecast tornadoes. "Furious" is narration colour for that grilling.
- They worked around the clock through Air Force files and tornado data looking for the pattern (DVIDS). Narration:
  "dig through old tornado records".
- 25 March, after lunch: "virtually the same weather pattern developing that had occurred March 20" (DVIDS, NWS).
- Odds: Miller later: "The chances of a tornado hitting the same spot five days later must have been astronomical. It
  must have been billions to one." (DVIDS). Fox Weather: "infinitesimal".
- Borum at the radar scope: "Are we going to have another tornado or not? Yes or no?" (DVIDS). Fox Weather also has
  "Are you going to issue a tornado forecast?" and "You are about to set a precedent." Narration shortens it to
  "Another tornado. Yes or no?"
- The forecast went out at 2:50 pm (DVIDS, NWS marker; weather.com says 3 pm): Fawbush wrote it, Miller typed it.
- Precautions (DVIDS, Fox Weather): aircraft pushed into hangars, doors and windows shut, parked planes tied down,
  loose objects cleared, incoming flights diverted, swing-shift workers sent to shelters and solid buildings.
- Miller went home (DVIDS: 4:45 pm, calling it "abandoning ship"; other accounts: about 5:30 pm), so sure the forecast
  would fail that "he wondered if he could make a living as an elevator operator" (DVIDS).
- The tornado hit about 6 pm (DVIDS 5:58 pm, NWS 6:00 pm), "almost exactly at the same spot" (DVIDS); NWS: within 100
  yards of the 20 March path. $6 million (NWS $6.1 million); 84 planes hit, 35 beyond repair; no injuries (Wikipedia,
  Fox Weather, weather.com) or one slight injury (NWS, DVIDS). Narration only says "This time, they were ready."
- "The first official tornado forecast" (Wikipedia, 1948 Tinker AFB tornadoes; NWS: "verified successfully for the
  first time ever"). "Official" matters: John Finley made experimental tornado predictions in the 1880s.
- "And tornado warnings were born": NWS calls the 1948 forecast "the forerunner of today's national severe weather
  forecasting"; the Weather Bureau lifted its tornado-warning ban in 1950 and set up its severe weather unit in 1952;
  Fawbush and Miller's method verified 67 of 75 forecasts and became the Air Force Severe Weather Warning Center (1951).
- Narration names nobody (keeps the hook fast); Tinker AFB, Fawbush, Miller and Borum go in the post description.
  Staging only: who plays whom, the office, the map, the radar, the elevator daydream, the look of the planes.
