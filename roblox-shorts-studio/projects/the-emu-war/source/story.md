## The facts (sources below)
- Western Australia's Wheatbelt (Campion district), late 1932: WWI veterans farming marginal land; about 20,000 emus
  migrated onto the farms, ate the wheat and broke fences (Wikipedia; Australian Geographic).
- The settlers asked Defence Minister Sir George Pearce for machine guns; soldiers would use them. Major G.P.W.
  Meredith, 7th Heavy Battery RAA, with Sgt S. McMurray and Gunner J. O'Halloran, two Lewis guns, 10,000 rounds.
  "Two gunners" = his two men.
- 2 Nov 1932: about 50 emus sighted out of range; when the farmers tried to herd them, the birds split into small
  groups and ran (Wikipedia; AG). 4 Nov: an ambush at a dam; more than 1,000 emus approached; the gun jammed (after
  about 12 birds). "Day three" = 4 Nov counting 2 Nov as day one.
- The major mounted a gun on a truck; the truck couldn't keep up and the ride was too rough to aim (Wikipedia; AG).
- Army observers said each mob had "its own leader... a big black-plumed bird which stands fully six feet high and
  keeps watch" (Wikipedia, citing a 1953 account). Narration: "a lookout... keeps watch while the others eat".
- By 8 Nov about 2,500 rounds had been fired; estimates of birds killed range from 50 to 200 (of ~20,000), so "Most
  of the emus aren't" holds. The House of Representatives discussed it; an MP asked, to laughter, whether a medal
  would be struck (Wikipedia). The troops were withdrawn on 8 Nov.
- Meredith: "If we had a military division with the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds it would face any army in
  the world... They can face machine guns with the invulnerability of tanks." Narration paraphrases the second line.
- Not in the narration: a second operation (13 Nov - 10 Dec) claimed 986 birds (disputed); the farmers' later requests
  for troops were refused and bounties and fences were used instead. "The emus won" is the common verdict on the
  operation (the army withdrew; the emus stayed). The post copy mentions the second attempt.
