On 17 July 1938, pilot Douglas Corrigan took off from Floyd Bennett Field in Brooklyn at dawn. His flight plan said Long Beach, California. He flew east instead.

For years the aviation authorities had refused to let him fly the Atlantic: his nine-year-old Curtiss Robin, bought for $310 and rebuilt by hand, was judged unsound for the crossing. So he filed a plan for a nonstop flight home to California, took off, and kept going into the clouds. He had no radio and a 20-year-old compass. When fuel began leaking into the cockpit, he punched a hole through the floor with a screwdriver so it would drain away from the hot exhaust.

28 hours and 13 minutes later he landed at Baldonnel Aerodrome near Dublin, Ireland, and reportedly said: "Just got in from New York. Where am I?" His explanation: he'd misread his compass in the clouds.

Officials sent a 600-word telegram listing the rules he'd broken. His pilot's certificate was suspended for 14 days, ending on 4 August, the day his ship brought him back to New York. More people turned out for his ticker-tape parade on Broadway than had cheered Charles Lindbergh. He was "Wrong Way" Corrigan for the rest of his life, and he never publicly admitted he'd meant to do it.
