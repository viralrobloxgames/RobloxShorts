## The facts (kept accurate; sources below)
- Monday 21 August 1911, about 7 a.m., the Louvre's closing day, so it was nearly empty. Vincenzo Peruggia, an
  Italian handyman who had worked at the Louvre (he helped fit protective glass cases, including the Mona Lisa's),
  wore one of the white smocks staff wore and was taken for a worker. (Some accounts say he hid in a storeroom
  overnight; Wikipedia says he came in that morning. The narration says neither.)
- He lifted the painting (77 x 53 cm, painted on a poplar panel) off its four iron pegs in the Salon Carre, carried
  it to a service staircase, and left the frame and glass case there.
- The service door was locked. A plumber, thinking he was an employee, unlocked it for him (Wikipedia). Some accounts
  say he had already removed the doorknob. He walked out; the narration doesn't say where he hid it on the way.
- Discovered the next day (Tuesday 22 August) by a painter, Louis Beroud, who came to paint/copy the gallery and
  found four empty pegs. Guards assumed it had been taken away to be photographed, which was routine; it took hours
  to confirm it was gone.
- The Louvre shut for a week. At least 60 police searched it (Wikipedia). The poet Guillaume Apollinaire was
  arrested and Pablo Picasso questioned (linked to earlier thefts of statuettes by an acquaintance); both cleared.
  The narration says "question Picasso", which holds for every account.
- Police visited Peruggia's room and questioned him; a detective wrote his report leaning on the table under which
  the painting was hidden in a cavity (Wikipedia). Other accounts say a trunk with a false bottom; "the table she's
  hidden in" is what Wikipedia describes.
- When the Louvre reopened, crowds came to stare at the empty space on the wall; the theft filled newspapers worldwide.
- Late 1913 he wrote to Florence art dealer Alfredo Geri as "Leonardo" and brought the painting to Florence; Geri
  and Uffizi director Giovanni Poggi checked it was genuine, took it for "safekeeping", then told the police, who
  arrested him at his hotel. Recovered December 1913, "over two years" after the theft; the narration says "two years
  later". Shown on screen only: he said he did it to return her to Italy; he got 1 year 15 days, served 7 months.
- The fame claim: National Geographic calls it "a lesser-known work" before the theft, which made it "a household
  name"; Wikipedia says it was famous before, but the theft "helped the artwork become one of the best known in the
  world". So the narration says "hardly anyone talked about" (it was not front-page news) rather than "nobody had
  heard of it", and "the most famous painting in the world" is about today, which no source disputes.
