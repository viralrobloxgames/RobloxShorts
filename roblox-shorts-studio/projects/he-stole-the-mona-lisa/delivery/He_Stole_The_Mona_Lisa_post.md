```
On Monday 21 August 1911, the Louvre's closing day, Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian handyman who had worked at the museum (he helped fit its paintings with protective glass), walked in wearing a white staff smock. He lifted the Mona Lisa off its four iron pegs, left the frame and the glass case on a service staircase, and when he found the door locked, a passing plumber opened it for him.

Nobody noticed until the next day, when a painter came to copy her and found four empty pegs. The guards assumed she had been taken away to be photographed. The Louvre shut for a week, about sixty police searched it, and the poet Guillaume Apollinaire was arrested and Pablo Picasso questioned (both were cleared). Police even visited Peruggia's room; a detective wrote his report leaning on the table she was hidden in.

When the museum reopened, crowds came to stare at the empty wall, and her face was in newspapers around the world. In December 1913 Peruggia took her to Florence and offered her to the art dealer Alfredo Geri, who had the Uffizi's director check she was real, then called the police.

She was famous among art lovers before 1911, but the theft put her on front pages everywhere, and today she is the most famous painting in the world.
```
