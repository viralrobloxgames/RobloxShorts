## The facts (kept accurate; sources below)
- 1995-96: Pepsi's "Pepsi Stuff" campaign let drinkers collect Pepsi Points for merchandise (T-shirt, shades, leather
  jacket). Its TV ad, first tested in the Pacific Northwest, ends with a teenager landing a Harrier jump jet outside
  his school; the caption reads "HARRIER FIGHTER 7,000,000 PEPSI POINTS"; the kid's line is "Sure beats the bus."
- The catalog's rules: an order needed at least 15 original Pepsi Points, and the rest could be bought for 10 cents
  a point. 7,000,000 points = $700,000.
- John Leonard, a 21-year-old business student (Shoreline Community College, Washington), noticed this. A Harrier
  was worth far more: the court cited about $37.4 million (other reports ~$33 million); the narration says "over
  thirty million", true for either figure.
- He got five investors (including Todd Hoffman, whom he knew from climbing) to front him the money.
- 28 March 1996: he sent 15 original points and a check for $700,008.50 (6,999,985 points at 10 cents plus $10
  shipping and handling); "seven hundred thousand dollars, shipping included" in the narration, the exact figure on
  the prop check.
- Pepsi returned the check with a letter calling the jet "fanciful", included only to make a "humorous and
  entertaining ad", plus coupons for free Pepsi.
- The case went to court (Pepsi filed in New York; Leonard brought his own suit, and the cases were heard as Leonard
  v. PepsiCo in the Southern District of New York). 5 August 1999: Judge Kimba Wood ruled for Pepsi: "no objective
  person could reasonably have concluded that the commercial actually offered consumers a Harrier Jet." Affirmed on
  appeal in 2000. He never got the jet.
- Pepsi changed the ad: the jet's price went to 700,000,000 Pepsi Points, with "just kidding" added on screen.
- Narration names only Pepsi (the story is about its ad, like Google in the Google story). Names (John Leonard, Todd
  Hoffman, Judge Kimba Wood, Harrier) go in the post description. Staging only (not claimed as fact): his room, the
  faces, the mailbox, the courtroom look, who the investors are.
