## The facts (kept accurate; sources below)
- On 2 July 1982, Larry Walters, a 33-year-old truck driver with no flight training, lifted off in a Sears lawn chair
  he called "Inspiration I", carried by 42 helium-filled weather balloons (8 ft across, from a military surplus
  store), from his girlfriend Carol Van Deusen's mother's backyard in San Pedro, Los Angeles (Smithsonian record;
  Wikipedia). Some retellings say 43 or 45 balloons and Guinness says 12 July; the Smithsonian says 42 and 2 July.
- The plan: rise on a tether (Air & Space magazine: "expecting to rise about 100 feet"; Guinness says about 300 ft)
  and float over the Mojave Desert. The cord tying the chair to his Jeep broke and he rose fast (Guinness: about
  800 ft a minute) to about 16,000 ft. Narration: "a hundred feet", "The rope snaps", "Sixteen thousand".
- At that height he met freezing temperatures and thin air (Wikipedia). Narration: "It's freezing."
- He had wanted to fly since he was 13, when he saw weather balloons hanging in a surplus store, and was unable to
  become a pilot because of his poor eyesight (Wikipedia; Ripley's says the Air Force turned him down for it).
  Narration: "He'd always wanted to fly, but his eyesight wasn't good enough" and "never allowed to be a pilot".
- He carried a parachute, a pellet (BB) gun, a CB radio, sandwiches, soft drinks, a camera and plastic jugs of water
  as ballast (Wikipedia; Guinness; the Smithsonian chair still has 13 gallon jugs tied on). Narration names the
  sandwiches and the pellet gun only. Not shown or said: the beer some accounts list.
- Pilots of TWA and Delta airliners on the approach to Los Angeles/Long Beach radioed air traffic control that they
  had passed a man in a lawn chair (Smithsonian record; Wikipedia). Narration: "Two airline pilots radio the tower:
  they've passed a man in a lawn chair." It does not repeat the popular "holding a gun" line (from embellished
  retellings).
- He shot several balloons to come down, taking care not to tip the chair (Guinness: seven), then accidentally
  dropped the gun overboard (Wikipedia; Air & Space). He came down slowly as helium leaked, dumping water to control it.
  Narration: "He pops a few, and starts to sink. Then he drops the gun."
- His dangling cables caught a power line in Long Beach, causing a 20-minute blackout in the neighbourhood; he landed
  unharmed and was arrested by Long Beach police waiting for him (Wikipedia). Guinness says the power was cut so he
  could be got down. Narration: "a whole neighbourhood goes dark", "He lands unhurt, and the police are waiting."
- Asked by reporters why he did it: "A man can't just sit around." (Air & Space magazine; Guinness).
- The FAA charged him with violations including operating an aircraft in an airport traffic area without two-way
  radio contact with the control tower; fined $4,000, reduced to $1,500 on appeal (Wikipedia; Smithsonian record:
  "three violations"). Narration: "He's fined for flying an aircraft without calling the tower" (no amount).
- He gave the chair to an admiring neighbourhood kid, Jerry Fleck, who decades later donated it to the Smithsonian;
  it is on display in the "Thomas W. Haas We All Fly" gallery of the National Air and Space Museum in Washington, DC
  (Smithsonian record; Air & Space magazine). Narration: "Today, his chair sits in the Air and Space Museum."
- Flight length is disputed (45 min in Wikipedia, about 90 min in Guinness and others); the narration gives none.
- Not in the narration, picture or post copy: his death in 1993 (off-tone and not needed). The museum shot never
  shows him there: the chair reached the museum long after he died.
